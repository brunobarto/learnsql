What is a database, really

Definition

Pick something

Click any header or row in the table on the left. The vocabulary of relational databases is small and precise — it repays ten minutes of attention.

Key

Primary key

A column (or set of columns) that is unique and never null. It names every row exactly once. store_key = 201 identifies one store, forever.

Reference

Foreign key

A column whose values point at another table's primary key. In fact_sales, store_key is a foreign key into dim_store. That single line is what makes a schema a schema.

Cardinality

One-to-many

One store holds many sales; each sale belongs to exactly one store. Drawing that relation as 1 → N is the whole art of the diagram on tab 03.

Three properties worth internalising

Redundancy costs.

If the string "Île-de-France" is written on ten million rows and the region is renamed, you have ten million updates and a chance of disagreement.

Constraints are documentation.

Types, keys and foreign keys are executable claims about the world. A database that accepts nonsense is a database that will contain nonsense.

Queries follow shape.

The number of joins a question needs is decided by the schema, not by the query writer. That is why star and snowflake differ in practice.

Star and snowflake: two answers to one question

Both are dimensional models: a central fact table holding measures and foreign keys, surrounded by dimension tables holding descriptive attributes. The question they answer differently is: what happens when a dimension itself contains redundancy?

Star — dimensions kept flat

dim_product carries every descriptive attribute, including the category, its department, the supplier and the supplier's country. One join reaches them all.

101Espresso machineKitchenHomeBarista Works
102Drip coffee makerKitchenHomeBarista Works

104Desk lampLightingHomeLumen Co

"Kitchen" repeats on rows 101–103. In a star schema that is accepted on purpose.

Snowflake — dimensions normalised further

The repeating attributes are pulled out into sub-dimensions. To reach a category you now chain fact → product → category.

101Espresso machine101

category_keycategorydepartment

"Kitchen" is written once. Depth replaces repetition.

Trade-offs, plainly

 Star schemaSnowflake schema
StorageMore redundancyLess redundancy, smaller dimension footprint
QueriesFewer joins, simpler SQLMore joins, longer SQL, more windows of error
MaintenanceOne row per attribute change; wider tablesChange a category once, affects everybody
Modeling originKimball dimensional styleThird normal form applied to dimensions
Typical enginesColumnar warehouses, BI semantic layersOperational cores, Inmon-style warehouses

Neither is correct in the abstract. The engine's cost of a join decides which one is cheap where you are running.

The snowflake metaphor

Draw the foreign-key graph and the shape appears: from the fact table, each dimension branches into further sub-dimensions, which may branch again. The outline is six-fold and crystalline rather than a single point of light. It is a diagrammatic coincidence that happens to be memorable — and it is unrelated to the cloud data warehouse that borrowed the name.
